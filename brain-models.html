About This Module

What Are the Brain Models?

Two handcrafted brain models with LED circuits visually demonstrate the neurological difference between a Typical Brain and an ADHD Brain. They help parents and children understand that ADHD is a brain-based neurological condition — not laziness or bad behaviour.

Typical Brain

The Typical Brain model glows with green LEDs across all regions, representing full neural connectivity. All areas — from the prefrontal cortex to the limbic system — light up together, showing coordinated brain activity.

Active regions:

Prefrontal Cortex Limbic System Basal Ganglia Cerebellum Sensory Cortex
ADHD Brain

The ADHD Brain model uses red LEDs in key areas such as the prefrontal cortex and limbic system, highlighting reduced activity in regions responsible for attention, impulse control, and emotional regulation.

Reduced-activity regions:

Prefrontal Cortex Limbic System Basal Ganglia
🧬

ADHD Is a Neurological Condition — Not a Choice

These models help parents and children understand that ADHD is caused by differences in brain structure and connectivity — particularly in the prefrontal cortex, which controls attention, impulse control, and decision-making. Children with ADHD are not being lazy or defiant; their brains are simply wired differently. Early understanding and the right support can transform outcomes.

Visual Learning

LED lights make abstract neuroscience concepts immediately visible and understandable to parents, students, and teachers at a science fair.

Reduces Stigma

When people see that ADHD is a brain-based difference — not laziness — it reduces stigma and encourages empathy and support for affected children.

Conversation Starter

The models spark meaningful conversations between parents, teachers, and healthcare professionals about seeking evaluation and support.

Neuroscience

Key Brain Regions Affected by ADHD

Prefrontal Cortex

Controls planning, attention, impulse control, and decision-making. Reduced activity here leads to difficulty focusing, forgetfulness, and acting before thinking.

Limbic System

Regulates emotions and motivation. Differences here contribute to emotional dysregulation — sudden frustration, low tolerance, and difficulty self-soothing.

Basal Ganglia

Involved in routine tasks, habit formation, and reward processing. When this region is underactive, children struggle to begin and complete everyday tasks automatically.
